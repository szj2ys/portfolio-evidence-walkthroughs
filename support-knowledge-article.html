The walkthrough task

A support portfolio can demonstrate more than polished writing. It can show how you defined the reader, corrected an unsafe assumption, and made an article easier to verify. This fictional packet concerns a missing export button in a made-up project-tracking application called Boardlight. The task is to walk through the article and its review notes while distinguishing content ownership from product behaviour. The example is not documentation for a real product. Do not follow its steps in another service or present its fictional verification notes as customer-support results.

The fictional artifact packet

S1 is an earlier sentence that assumes every user can export. S2 is the revised article excerpt. S3 records two fictional review cases and an unresolved product question. The packet is intentionally small enough to read aloud. It contains no real support tickets, names, account data, or claimed reduction in contacts. The exercise focuses on making a useful next step explicit without asking a reader to bypass permissions or send private screenshots.

S1 · Earlier wording

“Open any project and click Export. If the button is missing, ask someone to make you an owner.”

S2 · Revised fictional article excerpt

Why can’t I see Export?

Applies to: the fictional Boardlight web app, project CSV export. In this example, only project owners can export.

  1. Check that you opened the intended project, not the overview page.
  2. Check your project role in Members. If you are a viewer, a missing Export button is expected.
  3. If you need an export, ask the project owner whether they can provide the appropriate data. Do not request broader access only to make the button appear.
  4. If you are already an owner on the project page and Export is still missing, contact support with the project identifier, your role, and the time observed. Do not send project contents unless a separate approved process requires them.

Still unclear: this excerpt does not cover archived projects; support must confirm that case before adding an instruction.

Personal contribution boundaries

The fictional candidate drafted S2, removed the blanket access-upgrade suggestion, and organised the review cases. A product specialist supplied the role restriction and the location rule. A support reviewer tested the two stated scenarios in the fictional setting. The candidate did not implement permissions, approve data-access policy, or verify archived-project behaviour. The portfolio claim should be about the article revision and its review trail. “I fixed exports” would confuse a documentation change with a product change, while “I reduced tickets” would require outcome evidence absent from this packet.

A two-minute walkthrough

The intervals below total 120 seconds. Point to an artifact as you speak; the outline is a rehearsal guide, not a script to memorise or a claim that the fictional experience is yours.

  1. 0–15 seconds · Identify the reader and symptom

    “This article helps a reader who cannot see Export in the fictional Boardlight app. It distinguishes expected absence from a problem needing support.”

  2. 15–50 seconds · Walk the revised article

    Use S2 to show location, role, and the bounded escalation path. Explain why the article requests context without defaulting to private project contents.

  3. 50–75 seconds · Show the consequential revision

    Compare S1’s access-upgrade advice with S2’s request to ask the owner for appropriate data. The goal is a useful next step within the stated role boundary.

  4. 75–100 seconds · Trace the review evidence

    Point to the two checked cases in S3 and credit the specialist and reviewer. Keep the archived-project question visibly unresolved.

  5. 100–120 seconds · State the limit and maintenance need

    “The article covers these scenarios in this fictional packet. A real version needs an owner and review trigger when roles or export behaviour change.”

Skeptical follow-ups and reasoned answers

How do you know readers solved their problem?

The packet contains review cases, not reader-outcome evidence. It shows that the revised instructions are aligned with two described scenarios. A real outcome claim would need an appropriate feedback or task observation, and the result should not be inferred from publication alone.

Why not tell viewers to become owners?

S3 identifies role restriction as expected behaviour. S1’s advice jumps from needing a file to expanding access. S2 instead asks the owner whether they can provide the appropriate data, which addresses the need without assuming an access change is justified.

What would you do about archived projects?

The packet marks that case as unverified. I would ask the product specialist and test the relevant scenario through an approved route, then revise the article with the actual result. I would not improvise a workaround simply to remove the visible limitation.

Which part of the work demonstrates your contribution?

The change from S1 to S2 and the organised review trail show the fictional candidate’s writing and scoping decisions. Product rules came from a specialist, and scenario checks came from a reviewer. Those contributions are necessary evidence, not details to absorb into a solo-author story.

Try the exercise, then adapt it honestly

Ask a partner to play a viewer who only needs a copy of project data. Have them follow the article’s reasoning without clicking any real service. Then switch to an owner on an archived project and identify where the excerpt stops helping. For your own portfolio, choose a permitted article revision and show the reader problem, the changed instruction, and the review source. Remove customer details and internal system identifiers unless you have permission to share them. Practise explaining a limit without making the article sound useless.

What this packet cannot establish

Boardlight and its behaviour are invented. The article is not a universal procedure for export problems or account permissions. The review notes do not establish security, legal compliance, accessibility, or customer satisfaction. They illustrate how a writer can link instructions to a defined audience and review evidence. A real support article also needs maintenance ownership and a way to notice product changes. Keep those missing elements separate from the quality of the prose when discussing what you personally delivered.
