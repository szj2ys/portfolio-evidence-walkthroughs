The walkthrough task

Operations work can disappear behind a phrase such as “I improved the process.” This fictional packet gives you a more inspectable starting point: a community equipment-loan handoff from request to pickup. The exercise is to explain where responsibility changes, how an exception stays visible, and what a tiny case trace does not establish. A map is a model of intended work; a log shows recorded events in particular cases. Keep those two kinds of evidence separate rather than treating a tidy diagram as proof that the real operation always follows it.

The fictional artifact packet

The map, roles, identifiers, and traces are invented. O1 is the previous verbal handoff: a desk volunteer passes a request to a coordinator without a defined acknowledgment. O2 adds an explicit pending state and names the owner of the next action. O3 contains two illustrative case traces. One reaches pickup; the other stops at an exception. The incomplete case is retained so the walkthrough cannot honestly claim that every request was fulfilled.

Personal contribution boundaries

The fictional candidate mapped the ambiguous handoff, proposed the pending state, and wrote the owner labels. A coordinator defined what counts as availability confirmation. Desk volunteers tried the recording sheet in the two illustrative traces. The candidate did not create the equipment inventory, guarantee stock accuracy, or establish a service-level target. The packet supports explaining how the proposed handoff makes an owner visible. It does not support “I eliminated delays” or “all requests now succeed.” Name the coordinator’s contribution because the meaning of confirmation depends on their operational knowledge.

A two-minute walkthrough

The intervals below total 120 seconds. Point to an artifact as you speak; the outline is a rehearsal guide, not a script to memorise or a claim that the fictional experience is yours.

  1. 0–20 seconds · State the operating problem

    Point to the question mark in O1. Explain that the desk could not tell whether the request had been accepted for review, not merely whether the equipment was available.

  2. 20–50 seconds · Walk through the responsibility map

    Follow O2 from desk recording to coordinator acknowledgment. Name the owner at each transition rather than reading boxes without explaining the handoff.

  3. 50–80 seconds · Show the exception path

    Use the unavailable branch. Explain why the coordinator remains owner and why “not available” should not silently disappear from the queue.

  4. 80–105 seconds · Use the traces carefully

    Compare O3a’s pickup with O3b’s open exception. Show that the map can describe different outcomes, then name the missing closure evidence.

  5. 105–120 seconds · Close with your boundary

    “I proposed the handoff states and owner labels. These two traces illustrate use; they do not establish a general delay reduction or complete adoption.”

Skeptical follow-ups and reasoned answers

Did the new process make requests faster?

This packet does not provide a suitable before-and-after comparison or enough cases to answer that. The timestamps show sequence within two illustrative requests. I can explain the visible ownership change, but I cannot turn those timestamps into a general speed claim.

What happens if the coordinator never acknowledges?

O2 leaves that escalation unspecified. I would identify an agreed backup owner or review point with the team before calling the process complete. The diagram’s limitation is part of the walkthrough, not a detail to hide behind the successful L-14 trace.

Why is the unavailable request still open?

O3b records an exception but no alternative or closure. Keeping the coordinator as owner prevents the map from treating unavailability as automatic resolution. Whether that is the right operating rule would need agreement from the real team.

How do you know people followed the map?

The two traces are consistent with parts of O2, but they do not show every handoff or prove wider adoption. A real review would compare authorised case records with the intended steps and ask operators about workarounds. I would preserve mismatches instead of redrawing the map to conceal them.

Try the exercise, then adapt it honestly

Give a partner the map and ask them to trace a request that cannot be fulfilled. They should be able to name the current owner and the missing next decision. Then ask them to challenge the successful case: what happened outside the recorded events? For your own portfolio, pair a process diagram with one normal case and one exception, removing private operational details only with permission. Explain one ambiguity you found and one ambiguity that remains. That is more useful than presenting a flawless diagram with no record of how work actually moved.

What this packet cannot establish

The fictional process does not include stock reconciliation, damage checks, accessibility of pickup arrangements, escalation timing, or retention rules for borrower details. It is not an operating procedure for a real equipment service. The case timestamps are teaching data and should never be borrowed as your results. A diagram can demonstrate how you reason about work, but effectiveness, adoption, and completeness need their own evidence. Keep the final spoken claim as narrow as the packet you can show.
