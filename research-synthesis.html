The walkthrough task

A research portfolio can look persuasive while hiding the path from notes to conclusions. This exercise uses a tiny fictional packet about volunteers arranging shift swaps. The objective is to show how you organised observations, preserved a contradiction, and proposed a follow-up question. It is not to claim that three invented interviews represent a market or that a neat theme proves a product requirement. When adapting the exercise, respect the consent and disclosure limits attached to your own research. De-identifying a name alone does not necessarily make an interview safe to publish.

The fictional artifact packet

The three excerpts below are invented, not quotes from real participants. They describe different situations rather than three votes for one feature. R1 is a note from a volunteer who needs a replacement; R2 is from a coordinator who approves changes; R3 is from a volunteer who already has a reliable personal workaround. R4 is the analyst’s synthesis, kept separate from the notes so a reviewer can challenge the reasoning.

Personal contribution boundaries

In the fictional team, you conducted the conversations represented by R1 and R3, while a colleague interviewed the coordinator in R2. You organised the notes, wrote R4, and reviewed the interpretation with that colleague. You did not recruit a representative sample, build a scheduling tool, or test a solution. Your personal contribution is the traceable synthesis and the explicit unresolved question. Say that before describing “our research.” The role of the colleague matters because the approval dependency comes from their conversation, not yours.

A two-minute walkthrough

The intervals below total 120 seconds. Point to an artifact as you speak; the outline is a rehearsal guide, not a script to memorise or a claim that the fictional experience is yours.

  1. 0–20 seconds · State the research question and scope

    “We explored how three volunteers or coordinators described a shift swap. This is a small exploratory packet, not a prevalence estimate.”

  2. 20–50 seconds · Show two contrasting notes

    Point to R1’s uncertainty and R3’s working in-person arrangement. Let the contrast remain visible instead of presenting every note as support for a new app.

  3. 50–80 seconds · Trace one interpretation

    Use R2 to explain the distinction between acceptance and an updated rota. Show where that interpretation appears in R4 and what wording goes beyond a direct quote.

  4. 80–105 seconds · Name your contribution

    Describe which conversations you conducted, which a colleague conducted, and how you turned the combined notes into a reviewable synthesis.

  5. 105–120 seconds · Close with the unresolved check

    “Before proposing a workflow, I would ask who is allowed to approve a replacement and examine an actual handoff with permission.”

Skeptical follow-ups and reasoned answers

Why does this justify building a notification feature?

It does not yet. R1 suggests uncertainty, but the packet does not compare notification preferences or test whether notifications solve it. R3 also shows a working alternative. A reasonable next step is to investigate the unresolved handoff before selecting a feature.

Are you counting two quotes as a majority?

No. These are three illustrative situations, not a representative sample. R1 and R2 connect different roles in a process; they are not interchangeable votes. I would describe the connection and the sampling limit rather than calculate a percentage of users with a problem.

Did you ignore the participant who did not have a problem?

R3 remains in the synthesis as a counterpoint. It suggests that group size or meeting habits might affect the process. That is a hypothesis to explore, not a proven segment. Removing R3 would make the story cleaner but the reasoning less honest.

How can I tell which statements are yours?

R1–R3 are labelled note excerpts; R4 contains the analyst’s interpretation and proposed checks. In a real packet I would preserve authorised source identifiers and review notes so a colleague could trace or dispute the synthesis without relying on my presentation.

Try the exercise, then adapt it honestly

Ask a partner to choose one sentence in R4 and point to the note that supports it. Then ask them to rewrite the sentence so it claims too much. Repair that overstatement together. For your own portfolio, choose one modest finding and include a counterexample or uncertainty beside it. Explain what you did when sources disagreed. Do not replace that reasoning with a decorative cluster of sticky notes whose labels cannot be traced back to anything a participant actually said.

What this packet cannot establish

This packet contains no recordings, recruitment criteria, consent records, interview guide, or complete transcripts. Its quotations are invented teaching material, and the synthesis demonstrates a method rather than a real research outcome. A polished walkthrough does not establish research validity or permission to share source material. If real participant details are restricted, use an explicitly fictional reconstruction to explain the method and keep private research out of the public portfolio. Never imply that the reconstruction is a real customer study.
